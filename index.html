The Origin & Problem
How It Started

It started over tea. My mother is a government high school teacher with over two decades in the classroom. She mentioned that the Kerala government had made KTET mandatory for all teachers — for promotions, service extensions, even for experienced educators with thirty years of experience.

There was almost nothing to help them prepare. Not a single dedicated app. That was the brief I gave myself.

The Opportunity

KTET is a demanding exam covering pedagogy, child psychology, and subject knowledge. But the users who need to pass it aren't students — they're professionals managing full classrooms, households, and careers simultaneously.

Existing options — books, YouTube, offline tutors — were designed for a different kind of learner. None of it fit into a working teacher's day.

Competition
No direct competition.

No dedicated app existed for KTET preparation. Indirect alternatives included general study books, YouTube videos, and online tutors — none optimised for short, consistent daily sessions or for users aged 19–56.

This gap was the opportunity.

"There is no proper system to make the learning simple and personalised for these teachers. They need something quick — something they can do in short intervals of time."

— Problem Statement, KTET Master Research
User Research — What I Found
Methodology

Collected primary data from 20 teachers personally — B.Ed. students, mid-career government school teachers, and senior educators near retirement. I conducted the interviews myself, not through proxies, using a structured Google Forms questionnaire.

Key Findings

All participants needed something quick and bite-sized — usable in 20-minute windows. They wanted instant feedback on wrong answers, visible progress, and bilingual support (English + Malayalam). Motivation and consistency were the biggest barriers, not ability.

The Unexpected Challenge

The exam is recent so study material is genuinely scarce. I ended up manually sourcing past question papers and building the question bank from scratch — which forced me to understand the content deeply and made my design decisions sharper.

Key Design Decisions
D.01
Minimal layout designed for age 19 to 56
Clear hierarchy, generous touch targets, no experimental navigation. If a 54-year-old Deputy Headmistress can navigate it intuitively in a free period, it works.
D.02
Bilingual support — English and Malayalam from day one
Assuming all users are comfortable in English was a design failure waiting to happen. The language toggle is built into the core flow, not buried in settings.
D.03
Immediate feedback on wrong answers
Wrong answers highlighted in red, correct answer shown immediately. Learning happens at the moment of error — not in a review screen after ten questions.
D.04
Streak and XP mechanics grounded in motivation research
Added not because gamification is trendy, but because research showed teachers needed a reason to return consistently. Progress streaks reward persistence without making it feel like a children's app.
D.05
The "Master" character as a companion, not a mascot
The animated teacher character reacts to right answers, wrong answers, and streaks. This was a direct response to research showing users wanted to feel encouraged — not just assessed.

"The best UX solution isn't always the most sophisticated one. Sometimes it's the one that fits inside a twenty-minute tea break."

— Key Learning, KTET Master Project

Core Fixes Made
Why This Existed
Personal Motivation

I'm a cinephile. Amazon Prime Video has some of the best regional and international content in India. It also has one of the most frustrating interfaces I regularly encounter. I stopped complaining and started researching.

The Constraint
Brand identity stays intact.

Prime Video's deep blue, the smile logo, the subscription model, the content catalogue — none of that was on the table. This was a UX intervention, not a rebrand. The goal was to reduce friction, not redesign the product.

The Stakes

One participant had cancelled their subscription entirely — not because of the content library, but because of the interface. That detail made the stakes of the redesign concrete: poor UX has real business consequences.

Research Findings
01
Discovery is broken
Users consistently described the home screen as overwhelming and impersonal. Suggestions felt random. Finding a film to watch took significantly longer than it should — and many users gave up.
02
X-Ray is intrusive by default
X-Ray — Prime's cast and trivia overlay — was designed as a feature but users experienced it as an interruption during viewing. Most wanted to opt in, not opt out.
03
Player controls fight instinct
Controls were scattered. The 10-second skip button conflicted with how users naturally interact on phones and laptops. Multiple users had developed personal workarounds.

Prime Video — Player & Home Screen Redesign

Player Redesign — PC & Mobile
PC Player

All controls moved to the bottom (where eyes naturally rest). The 10-second skip became a double-click gesture. Speed and audio options grouped into a single expandable icon. X-Ray demoted from default overlay to opt-in info button. Hovering controls — appear when needed, disappear when not.

Mobile Player

Swipe up/down on the left side controls brightness. Same gesture on the right controls volume — a pattern users already know from YouTube. Double-tap anywhere seeks 10 seconds. A screen lock button prevents accidental pauses when the phone tilts.

"Sometimes good design is invisible. The best version of this redesign is one where users don't notice anything changed — they just stop getting frustrated."

— Key Insight, Amazon Prime Redesign

Understanding the User
Who Uses This

The primary audience was farmers — many accessing the internet on a basic Android phone, sometimes in poor connectivity, sometimes for the first time. Not tech-savvy investors. Not startup enthusiasts. Farmers.

That single insight changed almost every design decision I made.

The Design Filter

Every choice was filtered through one question: will a farmer in his forties, standing in a field, looking at this on a mid-range Android phone, immediately understand what this company does and how to reach them?

Fancy animations, dense content, multi-level navigation — all eliminated.

Client Requirements

Four things the client needed: every service clearly explained, photos and videos of real work, a direct quote request form (no phone call required), and a brand that feels professional across every touchpoint — website, print, and in-store.

What Was Delivered
01
Logo — Water, growth, and reliability
The mark integrates a sprouting plant with a subtle water reference. Clean, recognisable, and scalable from a favicon to a 6-foot printed banner.
02
Website — Minimal navigation, direct conversion flow
Homepage leads with a full-bleed aerial shot of irrigated farmland. Navigation: Home, About, Services, Team, Contact — no dropdowns, no hidden menus. A direct quote form eliminates the friction of finding and dialling a number.
03
Print system — In-store boards, posters, flyers
Every touchpoint uses the same typography, colour palette, and layout language. A farmer seeing a Sprouts flyer at an agri-supply shop should immediately recognise the same brand from the website.
04
Consistency as trust
For a company serving farmers, trust is everything. Consistency across website, print, and in-store materials communicates that Sprouts is a professional, reliable operation — which directly supports how they win business.

"Working with a real client means navigating real constraints. That responsibility sharpens your thinking considerably."

— Reflection, Sprouts Enterprises Project

Context & Methodology
Background

My undergraduate major was Commerce with a specialisation in Finance. For my final-year project, I chose to research NFTs — simultaneously confusing half the world and captivating the other half. The formal title: A Study on Investors' Attitude and Perception Towards NFT Investment.

Research Method

A structured questionnaire distributed to NFT artists and investors through online communities and direct outreach. Primary data collected, cleaned, and analysed statistically. Findings visualised through charts and compiled into a formal written report.

The Hard Part

Finding the right participants was harder than the research itself. NFT users were scattered across Discord servers and crypto communities. Many participants had bought NFTs as collectibles, not investments — which required more careful questionnaire design to surface useful data.

Key Findings
01
Collectibles over investments
The majority of respondents were buying NFTs for aesthetic or cultural value, not financial return. The emotional driver was ownership of something unique — not portfolio diversification.
02
Risk awareness was low
Most participants had limited awareness of the financial risks involved. Many couldn't distinguish the difference between platforms or articulate why they chose OpenSea (56%) over alternatives.
03
Physical assets still win
Compared directly with gold, real estate, or equities, interest in NFTs as a long-term investment vehicle was notably lower — even among active NFT buyers.
The UX Connection
What This Taught Me

User research and financial research require the same core skill: designing questions that elicit honest, useful data — then finding patterns in human behaviour and communicating them clearly to people who weren't in the room.

This project built those instincts before I knew what to call them.

How It Shows Up in UX

The rigour I developed here — structuring a questionnaire, anticipating participant bias, analysing qualitative data alongside quantitative charts — shows up directly in how I approach user research for every design project.

It made me a more careful, more thorough researcher.

"Analysing how people behave around a product and turning that into a visual story is exactly what UX research does. This project built those instincts before I knew what to call them."

— Reflection, NFT Research Project
